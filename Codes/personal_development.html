Personal Development

My Reading Reflections

Book: The Richest Man in Babylon
Author: George S. Clason

Chapters

  • Seven Cures for a Lean Purse
  • Five Laws of Gold
  • My Biggest Takeaways

Seven Cures for a Lean Purse

The Seven Cures for a Lean Purse stood out to me because many of the lessons relate to financial habits that I have developed over the years, while others highlighted areas where I still have room to improve.

The first cure, "Start thy purse to fattening," encourages saving at least one-tenth of what you earn. I do not currently save a fixed ten percent of my income, but saving has been important to me since my first job. Even when I was earning much less, I opened a Golden Harvest savings plan and saved what I could afford. As my income increased, I would not say that my entire lifestyle changed with it. I still enjoy buying things that I want or need, but I try to stay within my budget and continue saving and investing.

I believe some of my attitude towards money comes from watching my mother. I have always known her to be independent, and that is something I have always wanted for myself. One of my biggest fears is being broke and having to depend on someone else financially. I never want to be in a position where I have to wait for someone else to provide for me if I can help it. Because of that, I tend to be extremely cautious with my money. I will spend and enjoy my money, but I also think about the security that comes from having savings of my own.

"Control thy expenditures" is especially relevant to me now. I have been working more seriously on budgeting and looking at where my money goes. I have cancelled subscriptions that I no longer need, and I realized that I was spending quite a bit of money ordering food. I have therefore been trying to cook and meal prep more instead of constantly buying food outside. I also dislike carrying debt. At one point, I had accumulated credit card debt and watching the balance increase as interest was added bothered me. I eventually decided that it made more sense to use the money to clear the balance in one large payment and then return my focus to saving and investing.

"Make thy gold multiply" is another cure that I can relate to. I opened an investment account several years ago and began investing in stocks. Before that, I had used different savings plans, but I realized that some of my money was earning very little. Seeing one of my stock investments increase significantly showed me the difference between simply putting money aside and putting some of my money to work for me. It also made me wish that I had understood investing earlier and had been in a position to invest more.

I can also relate strongly to "Make of thy dwelling a profitable investment." While working in Montego Bay, I was paying rent even though my father owned a house that was not being occupied. One day I actually sat down and calculated how much I was paying in rent, and the amount was alarming. I had accumulated money through a savings plan at work, so I decided to use that money to make the house livable and move home rather than continue giving that money to a landlord every month. I am also taking steps towards acquiring land, so the broader lesson of using money to acquire assets rather than spending everything on things with no lasting value is something I am continuing to work towards.

Preparing for the future is also important to me. I have pension and life insurance arrangements in place, and I have encouraged my father to think about these things as well because I am an only child and I understand that future financial responsibilities could otherwise fall on me.

Finally, "Increase thy ability to earn" may be one of the cures that best describes where I am in my life now. I already have a job, but I want a career. Pursuing my degree in Computer Networking and Cybersecurity is therefore an investment in myself. I also intend to pursue professional certifications that can increase my knowledge, make me more marketable and improve my earning potential.

Five Laws of Gold

What stood out to me most in the Five Laws of Gold was Arkad's decision not to simply hand his wealth to his son, Nomasir. Instead, he gave him an opportunity to prove that he could learn how to manage wealth. I actually agree with that approach. I have seen situations where one generation worked extremely hard to build a successful life, only for a younger generation to have a completely different attitude towards money. If someone receives wealth without learning how it was created, protected and grown, it can be very easy to squander what another person spent years building.

Nomasir's mistakes also made this chapter particularly relatable to me. When I was younger, a schoolmate introduced me to what appeared to be agreat opportunity to make money. I knew very little about that type of business, but the presentation made the opportunity look attractive and profitable. I even took out a $50,000 loan to get involved. In the end, I made no money from it. Instead of earning the income I had imagined, I basically ended up with products sitting at home, including coffee that my mother eventually drank.

It is funny to think about now, but financially it taught me an important lesson. I had focused on how much money I was told I could make without first understanding how the business actually worked. I later became involved in another similar opportunity and, although I made a little money from that one, I eventually understood much more about how these business models operated. I learned that seeing other people displaying success does not mean that I should immediately put my own money into something.

That experience connects strongly with the laws about protecting your money and seeking advice from people who are knowledgeable about the investment being considered. Today, I am much more interested in understanding where my money is going, how an investment works and what the risks are before I commit to it. There will always be people willing to take advantage of someone who is inexperienced or attracted by the promise of quick money. For me, avoiding that requires knowledge and being willing to ask questions before investing.

My Biggest Takeaways

My biggest takeaway from both chapters is that receiving or earning money does not automatically create financial security. What matters is what you know and what you do with the money once it reaches your hands. I already save and invest, avoid unnecessary debt and try to live within my budget, but these chapters reminded me that good financial habits have to be intentional and consistent.

I also believe in learning from people who have achieved what I am trying to achieve. I have someone in my life who has been very successful with investing, and I have watched how he repeatedly finds ways to make his money work for him. I keep that person close and ask for financial advice because I recognize the value of learning from someone with experience rather than trying to figure out everything on my own.

At present, one of my biggest investments is myself. My main focus is completing my degree and positioning myself for a career rather than simply having a job. I have also explored other ways of increasing my income, including obtaining my real estate licence and doing some social media management on the side. Going forward, I want to continue saving, investing and learning so that the money I work for can help me build the independence and financial security that have always been important to me.
